# Flagship Box — Formalizing Conceptual Development and Discovered Transfer

A machine-mediated inquiry can change more than its beliefs. It can also change the conceptual system in which scientific questions are expressed. We represent a concept state as

\[
C_t=(\Sigma_t,R_t,S_t,O_t,I_t,E_t,X_t,P_t,K_t),
\]

where `Sigma` denotes primitives/symbols, `R` relations, `S` scope, `O` operational links, `I` invariants, `E` exemplars, `X` counterexamples/anomalies, `P` parent concepts and `K` provenance/authority constraints. Conceptual development is a versioned proposal

\[
\tau:C_t\rightarrow C_{t+1}
\]

such as specialization, generalization, split, merge, bridge, reparameterization, revision or deprecation.

A transition does not earn scientific credit because it creates a cleaner vocabulary. It must recover the strongest applicable parent, preserve registered old-valid cases, bind its losses and either change a prospective protected decision/prediction or establish a formally checked necessity.

Transfer into the new concept state is likewise not a free analogy. For source-bound structures `S_D` and `S_T`, a candidate transfer is a partial typed map

\[
\phi:\mathcal S_D\rightharpoonup\mathcal S_T
\]

that is challenged for type/relation preservation, invariants, counterexamples, measurement semantics and authority. In formal subdomains this may reduce to homomorphism, anti-unification, concept-lattice closure, functoriality or countermodel search; in empirical science the decisive witness remains an independently evaluable scientific consequence.

The field-level hypothesis is therefore not that one mathematical formalism describes every science. It is that a machine can discover which parent structure is relevant, test what survives translation and change its conceptual representation only when the resulting scientific transition earns a protected residual beyond the strongest native parents.
