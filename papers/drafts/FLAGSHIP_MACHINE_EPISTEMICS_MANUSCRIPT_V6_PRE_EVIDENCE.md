# Machine Epistemics: Toward an Absorptive Science of AI-Driven Inquiry

**Perspective manuscript V6 — pre-evidence journal-length draft**  
**Target:** Nature Machine Intelligence Perspective  
**Status:** argument and surface revision after the F0–F3 foundation freeze. This draft does not claim that Machine Epistemics is already founded, that F2 is superior, or that the specialist evidence is complete.

## Abstract

Artificial intelligence is moving from isolated scientific prediction toward persistent research processes that retrieve literature, construct models, choose experiments, operate software and instruments, interpret results and revise scientific claims. The knowledge needed to govern these processes already exists across formal epistemology, control, statistics, causal inference, measurement, numerical analysis, assurance, cognitive science, social epistemology and scientific practice. The unresolved problem is how their outputs and validity conditions interact when one executable research episode crosses those disciplinary boundaries. We propose **Machine Epistemics** as a falsifiable higher-order field hypothesis: native parent theories remain recoverable within a typed federation, while cross-parent transition constraints aim to explain and improve how machine-mediated science changes what it knows, how it represents a problem, and what it chooses to investigate. We distinguish this proposal from a strongest-parent federation, selective interfield theories and a domain-only federation, and define the prospective evidence required to prefer any one of them. The programme succeeds only if it preserves parent-native validity, generates new independently testable consequences and improves protected scientific decisions under matched resources. Otherwise, it should contract.

## Research is becoming an executable, state-changing process

Artificial intelligence has contributed to scientific prediction and data analysis for decades. The more consequential development is that substantial portions of inquiry are now assembled into executable systems. Autonomous chemistry and materials platforms connect planning, computation, active learning and instruments; multi-agent systems link literature search, hypothesis generation and analysis; and domain agents increasingly operate over persistent code, data and experimental artifacts [1–8]. These systems differ greatly in maturity, but they change what must be evaluated. The relevant object is no longer only a model output. It is a sequence of actions that can alter the state of an investigation.

That sequence can fail while every local component appears successful. A search agent can return genuine papers but bind a claim to the wrong passage. Several reviewers can agree because they inherit the same model or source. A numerically stable program can accurately solve a scientifically inadequate mathematical model. A benchmark can be perfectly reproducible yet unable to expose the failure implied by a broad claim. An abstraction can preserve one decision while destroying another. An exploratory observation can be narrated retrospectively as confirmatory evidence. A technically accessible dataset can remain unavailable for a proposed use because consent or collective authority is absent. And a machine can discover a powerful non-human strategy whose scientific interpretation or scope is not established by performance alone.

These are not simply software bugs or ordinary prediction errors. They are failures in **scientific-state transition**: a proposed change in what an investigation accepts, how it represents the problem, which prior results remain valid, or what action it is permitted to take.

We use **Machine Epistemics** for the proposed science of those transitions in AI-mediated and hybrid research. Its central question is:

> Under what conditions may a machine-mediated research process change a scientific commitment, representation, method or agenda—and which prior commitments must be preserved, reopened or left unresolved when it does?

The phrase denotes a field hypothesis, not a claim that the field has already been established.

## The parent sciences are the foundation material

Machine Epistemics has an unusually dense intellectual neighbourhood. Reliable inquiry and formal learning study convergence and identifiability [9,10]. Belief revision, truth-maintenance systems and model-based diagnosis study changing commitments, support and causes of discrepancy [11–13]. Decision theory, metareasoning and Bayesian experimental design study which action or computation to perform under uncertainty and resource limits [14,15]. Control and cybernetics study feedback, regulation, observability and fault response [16]. Causal transportability, measurement invariance and metrology study when scientific judgments survive changes of population, instrument or representation [17–19]. Abstract interpretation, simulation relations and formal verification establish properties across computational representations [20].

Statistics supplies much more than final estimators. Statistical workflow includes model construction, implementation, checking, sensitivity, comparison and troubleshooting; simulation-based calibration can test whether an inference program produces the posterior implied by an encoded model [21,22]. Numerical analysis distinguishes model adequacy, problem conditioning, discretization, algorithmic stability, floating-point behaviour and validated enclosures. Scientific-software testing addresses the oracle problem through exact, partial, differential, statistical and metamorphic checks. Severe-testing traditions ask whether a procedure could have exposed the error that a scientific claim denies [23].

Provenance and reproducibility expose lineage but do not establish scientific correctness [24]. Dependence-aware evidence synthesis and robustness analysis ask whether apparently different confirmations share assumptions, data or instruments. Assurance cases and formal argumentation connect evidence, premises, defeaters and bounded claims; an accepted argument remains conditional on its premises and rules [25,26]. Distributed-systems theory distinguishes agreement, safety, liveness and application validity, showing why multi-agent consensus is not scientific truth [27].

Human inquiry adds further constraints. Pragmatist accounts treat inquiry as arising from genuine doubt and problematic situations and allow the problem formulation itself to change [28,29]. Knowing-how, tacit skill, situated action and distributed cognition show why explicit statements can underdetermine competent scientific performance [30–33]. Metacognition distinguishes task performance from monitoring of performance [34]. Social epistemology emphasizes testimony, criticism, background assumptions and the uptake of objections [35]. Historical and sociological work on experiment, instruments, boundary objects and epistemic cultures shows that material systems and local practices shape what can be observed and compared [36–38]. Indigenous data-governance work further separates machine accessibility from consent, collective benefit and authority to control [39].

Machine-native intelligence supplies another parent set. Automated discovery, theorem proving, program synthesis, self-play, evolutionary search and recent program-search systems demonstrate that useful machine strategies need not imitate a human verbal reasoning trace [40–43]. That result is important but limited: difference from humans is not evidence of scientific validity, and higher compute is not automatically higher intelligence.

The presence of these parents does not invalidate a new field. Interfield theories are scientifically valuable precisely when mature fields address different aspects of one domain but lack a tested account of their relationship [44]. The challenge is therefore not to find an empty territory. It is to determine whether the valid knowledge of these sciences can be absorbed into a higher-order theory that preserves their local strengths, explains their relations and generates consequences unavailable from their strongest federation.

## Four competing foundations

A credible field proposal must compare more than one interpretation of the same knowledge surface.

### F0: strongest parent federation

On the first interpretation, no new theory is needed. Mature methods remain in their native representations and are connected through strong workflow, data and expert-routing interfaces. Scientific value comes from selecting and composing the correct parent: metareasoning for action, causal transport for reuse, metrology for comparability, VVUQ for computation, assurance for claims, governance for permission, and so on.

F0 is not a strawman. It is the lower bound a higher theory must exceed. It has high native fidelity and benefits from mature algorithms. Its possible weakness is that cross-parent failures can fall between local interfaces, leaving programme-level closure, reopening and validity composition to ad hoc arbitration.

### F1: selective interfield theories

The second interpretation accepts that relations among selected parent fields are scientific objects. Typed bridge theories can connect execution and evidence, transport and revalidation, uncertainty and action, exploration and confirmation, or scientific support and authority. F1 can produce genuine explanations and tests without claiming one universal theory of inquiry.

Its risk is bridge proliferation. Pairwise theories may not compose, and the result can become a catalogue of adapters rather than a coherent programme.

### F2: absorptive supertheory

The third interpretation treats machine-mediated inquiry as a class of state-changing scientific systems. Parent sciences remain recoverable native views inside a typed federation. A small set of cross-parent constraints then aims to explain how a scientific transition is identified, warranted, transported, invalidated, reopened and closed.

F2 is the aspirational Machine Epistemics theory. It does not replace local science. It must explicitly defer when a specialised parent is sufficient and more efficient. Its distinctive burden is prospective: it must generate valid bridge relations, failure boundaries, experiments or decisions beyond F0.

### F3: domain federation without universal transition laws

The fourth interpretation preserves domain-native methods and authority while allowing minimal interoperability through source identity, boundary objects and translation receipts. It denies that stable cross-domain transition laws exist.

F3 is a serious pluralist control. It is preferable to F2 whenever the common abstraction erases native meanings or creates misleading coherence.

## Candidate constraints on scientific-state change

The final theory should contain fewer propositions than the parent atlas. The following are candidate constraints, not settled laws.

### Bound transition identity

A scientific transition must bind what changed: the subject, problem and criterion, source and evidence, representation or evaluator epoch, and authority scope. Silent mutation cannot inherit the old decision automatically. Legitimate calibration or conceptual iteration is possible, but it requires an explicit relation to previous states and a record of which claims remain comparable.

### Warrant factorization and non-amplification

Scientific support can depend on several distinguishable links: source identity, semantic interpretation, model adequacy, numerical or software validity, oracle or test adequacy, argument structure, transport and authority. These links fail differently. A valid provenance chain does not prove the model; a stable algorithm does not validate the scientific representation; a strong statistical result does not create permission to act.

The candidate higher-order claim is that the scope of an adopted transition cannot exceed its materially weakest necessary link, and one link cannot silently authenticate another.

### Typed conservation through composition

Relation family, direction, approximation, uncertainty form, dependence and translation loss must remain visible as results move across systems. An approximate transport cannot become exact merely by joining a longer chain. Ignorance cannot be converted into a precise probability without an additional assumption. Several dependent confirmations cannot become independent through agent count.

This constraint is deliberately formal in spirit but plural in implementation. Causal, measurement, behavioural, procedural and authority relations remain different native objects.

### Error-detection adequacy and selective reopening

A pass supports only errors the evaluator or oracle had a warranted capacity to reveal. When evidence, computation, relation or evaluator validity later fails, only commitments whose complete sufficient support is lost should reopen. Independently supported conclusions remain standing.

Together, these ideas connect severe testing and software validation with truth maintenance and belief revision. Their value must be demonstrated against a strong parent composition, not inferred from conceptual elegance.

### Exploration is not confirmation

Exploratory and constructive actions can expand the observable or conceptual state of inquiry. They may create an instrument, representation, dataset, model system or problem formulation needed for later science. Their outputs do not thereby confirm an explanation. A controlled research process should preserve the distinction among exploratory, discriminative, confirmatory, calibrative, constructive and replicative actions.

This proposition also limits obligation-driven control: the current obligation vocabulary cannot define every legitimate future scientific action, because some actions construct the state in which a better obligation can be expressed.

### Parent recovery and local deference

For every parent theory \(P_i\), a higher theory \(T\) should provide an embedding and a context-bound recovery:

\[
P_i \xrightarrow{\,\mathrm{embed}_i\,} T,
\qquad
T|_{D_i} \xrightarrow{\,\mathrm{recover}_i\,} P_i.
\]

Critical native judgments should be preserved unless explicit counterevidence or a scope change justifies revision. A broad theory that cannot recover local scientific competence is an overgeneralization. A higher layer that activates on every simple case is a performance drag.

### Machine-native internal freedom with an external scientific witness

Machine cognition may use programs, latent states, graphs, populations, parallel search or intermediate languages unlike human reasoning. Requiring a natural-language chain at every step would constrain the machine to one human interface rather than scientific validity.

The corresponding limit is that adoption of a scientific transition requires an external witness proportionate to the claim: relevant source and evidence, assumptions, uncertainty, validity checks, relation to prior commitments and authority. This is not a demand to expose every internal activation. It is a demand that non-human computation remain scientifically accountable at the transition boundary.

### Bounded closure

No finite system can establish exhaustion of all possible knowledge. Closure is relative to a declared search and evaluation universe, its censored routes, residual uncertainty and omission challenges. A material new parent, source mode, evaluator or domain can reopen the terminal. `CANNOT_CHECK` remains a scientifically meaningful state.

## What evidence could found the field?

A field cannot be founded by definitions alone. F0–F3 must be instantiated prospectively and evaluated on the same cases, information and resources.

The strongest current autonomous-science benchmarks reinforce this need. End-to-end rediscovery systems still show substantial variance and failures in experimental design, execution and evidence-supported conclusion, while scientific-literature discovery remains far from comprehensive [45,46]. The relevant evaluation should therefore not be paper fluency or agent count. It should include false completion, unsafe transport, source and computation validity, calibrated uncertainty, selective reopening, useful discriminator generation, resource cost and correct refusal.

A prospective programme should include cases such as: consensus on a reproducible but scientifically wrong model; calibration iteration that preserves some historical claims and reopens others; exploration needed before a useful hypothesis can be stated; action under imprecise or conflicting uncertainty; ontology transport with a decisive local constraint; governed data that are technically accessible but not authorized for the proposed use; a machine-native strategy requiring an external scientific witness; and robustness analysis in which apparently diverse methods share one hidden assumption.

Before outcomes are revealed, each foundation candidate should propose the likely hidden failure, a discriminator, expected possible results and claim consequences. This turns **generativity** into an observable scientific outcome rather than a retrospective narrative.

The comparison should be a partial order rather than one score. Hard failures—source corruption, criterion drift, unsafe transport, invalid uncertainty strengthening, authority violation or critical parent-capability loss—are non-compensatory. Among hard-valid systems, the evaluation should consider native fidelity, scientific-decision quality, prospective generativity, cross-domain transfer, explanatory integration, robustness, computational and human cost, auditability and extensibility.

F2 earns only a provisional absorptive-supertheory status if it is no worse than F0 on native parent controls, strictly better on at least one protected cross-parent problem, produces an independently evaluated generative or integrative gain, survives more than one domain, and correctly remains inactive when a parent or simple method is sufficient. F1, F0 and F3 remain valid outcomes. A field-level claim should contract if they are sufficient.

## Machine intelligence need not converge on human thought

Human cognition is an indispensable source of hypotheses, failures and collaboration design, but no evidence shows that it is the universally optimal computational architecture. Human inquiry is shaped by biological memory, attention, communication, embodiment, institutional history and limited parallelism. Machine systems have different affordances: exact replay, large external memory, program execution, parallel hypothesis portfolios, synthetic experience and representations that need not be linguistically natural.

Machine Epistemics should therefore evaluate cognition by scientific consequences rather than resemblance. A human strategy may be best in one task because it carries rich tacit and contextual knowledge. A machine-native strategy may be better elsewhere because it can search a formal space or preserve many alternatives without narrative collapse. A hybrid may be strongest when humans supply context and legitimate authority while machines supply search, memory and computation.

The field’s role is not to prescribe one mind. It is to make the scientific commitments created by different minds comparable, testable, revisable and authority-bounded.

## Boundaries and outlook

Machine Epistemics does not currently claim machine consciousness, a universal theory of intelligence, one universal scientific ontology or an algorithm that replaces domain science. It does not treat every recipe, testimony or unexpected event as scientific evidence. It does not infer novelty from incomplete retrieval or authority from confidence. It does not claim saturation of human civilization.

Its stronger aspiration is an absorptive science: one that takes the mature sciences of inquiry seriously enough to preserve their native results, yet moves upward by explaining and testing the relations among them. The success of that programme should be judged by what it predicts, which failures it exposes, which scientific decisions it improves, and where it correctly defers.

If F2 survives those tests, Machine Epistemics could become a higher-order science of machine-mediated inquiry. If only selected bridges survive, the correct result is an interfield theory. If the strongest parent federation already suffices, the contribution is rigorous integration engineering. If native scientific practices resist common transition laws, a domain federation is the honest outcome.

The name is therefore less important than the experiment. AI-driven science needs not only increasingly capable agents, but a tested account of when their actions are allowed to change what science claims to know.
