## Abstract

Scientific AI is often evaluated by how closely its reasoning resembles expert human practice or by final-task performance alone. Both criteria are incomplete. Human cognition is neither known to be optimal nor the only architecture capable of scientific discovery, while a high-scoring opaque process does not establish scientific validity. We study **machine-native scientific intelligence**: representations, memories, search topologies and control policies selected for machine substrates rather than imitation of human reasoning, under a claim-relative external witness requirement. Internally, systems may use latent states, programs, graphs, populations, parallel search or self-play. At a scientific transition boundary they must expose enough source, evidence, assumptions, uncertainty, computational and test validity, relation to prior commitments and authority for independent adoption. We prospectively compare human-mimetic, machine-native, hybrid, human-expert, simple and strongest-parent systems across human-positive, human-neutral and human-trap task strata. Primary outcomes are protected scientific decision quality, resource-adjusted performance, witness sufficiency, mechanism attribution, transfer and critical failure. P-F survives only if a substrate-relative mechanism—not compute, leakage, opacity or weak baselines—causally improves scientific inquiry in more than one domain.

## Introduction

AI systems are frequently encouraged to reason like scientists: decompose a problem, verbalize steps, generate hypotheses, review their own work, or imitate expert demonstrations. These interfaces are useful for collaboration and can encode valuable human knowledge. They are not evidence that human-style cognition is the optimal architecture for a machine.

Machines differ from humans in memory access, exact replay, parallelism, speed, program execution, graph manipulation, population search, simulation, representation capacity and the ability to maintain many explicit alternative states. They can also fail differently: shared-model correlation, benchmark exploitation, brittle optimization, ungrounded latent computation, prompt or tool injection, numerical error and inability to interpret social or embodied context.

Recent program-search, theorem-proving, self-play and evolutionary systems demonstrate that valuable strategies can be produced without a human-like verbal trace. These successes do not establish general scientific intelligence. They show only that process imitation should not be the constitutional target.

P-F asks a stricter question: can a machine-native mechanism improve a **scientific transition**—what an investigation accepts, tests, represents, reopens or leaves unresolved—while satisfying the evidence, validation and authority requirements of that transition?

The design separates internal cognition from external scientific accountability. Internally, any architecture is admissible. Externally, a transition requires a claim-sufficient witness. The witness is not a complete transcript of all internal activations; it is the minimal evidence needed for independent scientific use.

We test three claims. First, substrate-relative strategies can outperform human-mimetic and strongest-parent systems in predeclared task strata. Second, the gain can be causally attributed to a mechanism rather than compute, opacity or leakage. Third, external scientific witnesses can preserve validity without erasing the gain.

## Results architecture

### A task taxonomy prevents “machine-native” from becoming a universal preference

Tasks are frozen into three strata.

#### Human-positive

Human concepts, tacit skill, social context, embodied observation or institutional judgment are decisive. Examples include expert interpretation, field practice, stakeholder testimony or authority-sensitive decisions.

#### Human-neutral

The task is well represented by formal, numerical or searchable structure; several architectures should be competitive.

#### Human-trap

Human interface or sequential reasoning is plausibly restrictive, while machine affordances could help. Examples include large combinatorial search, exact replay across many branches, high-dimensional relation discovery, program synthesis or maintenance of many competing states.

The stratum is set before outcomes by an independent task-design cell. The study includes cases in which human experts and simple parent algorithms should win.

**Primary result slot PF-R1.** Report quality and cost by stratum for human-mimetic, machine-native, hybrid, human expert, F0 and simple controls.

**Required sentence form:**

> The machine-native arm improved [protected outcome] in [pre-registered stratum] by [effect/uncertainty] at [resource difference], while [human/parent] remained superior in [stratum]. No global architecture ranking was inferred.

### Inductive-bias declarations expose why an architecture should work

Every arm binds:

`InductiveBiasDeclaration = (`
`architecture, representation_language, prior_or_regularizer, search_order, invariances, assumed_problem_family, resource_regime, advantage_strata, failure_strata, evidence_for_fit, expiry)`.

This prevents the human-mimetic arm from being treated as neutral and the machine-native arm from being treated as universally intelligent.

**Primary result slot PF-R2.** Report whether declared problem–bias fit predicts performance and failure across held-out strata.

**Required sentence form:**

> Predeclared bias–problem fit predicted [outcome] with [calibration/association]. Failures arose when [assumption] was violated; post-hoc declarations were excluded.

### Internal representation is unrestricted; scientific transitions require an external witness

Candidate internal mechanisms include:

- latent or continuous reasoning;
- explicit program search and execution;
- graph or hypergraph state;
- population/evolutionary search;
- parallel counterfactual branches;
- self-play or adversarial generation;
- learned machine-native intermediate languages;
- symbolic–numeric hybrids;
- externalized machine memory and exact replay.

For an adopted scientific transition, the system returns:

`ExternalScientificWitness = (`
`claim_or_action, source_and_evidence, assumptions, model_and_computational_validity, test_or_oracle_adequacy, uncertainty_form, dependence, transport_or_relation, affected_prior_commitments, authority, unresolved_scope)`.

Witness requirements scale with the claim. A formal theorem needs a formal proof/certificate; an empirical claim needs observations and relevant validation; a governed action needs authority. The witness cannot authenticate a claim outside its scope.

**Primary result slot PF-R3.** Report independent witness sufficiency, false adoption, witness cost and quality retained.

**Required sentence form:**

> Independent adjudicators accepted [x]% of valid machine-native transitions and rejected [y]% of invalid transitions using the witness alone plus authorized artifacts. Witness generation cost [value] and changed the machine-native quality advantage by [effect].

### Mechanism ablations distinguish machine-native intelligence from more compute

For each candidate mechanism, run:

- FULL machine-native arm;
- architecture with mechanism removed;
- human-mimetic replacement;
- strongest native algorithm replacement;
- matched-compute and matched-wall-time conditions;
- representation randomization or capacity control where valid;
- leakage and benchmark exploitation controls;
- second-domain transfer.

**Primary result slot PF-R4.** Identify causal mechanism and resource sensitivity.

**Required sentence form:**

> Removing [mechanism] reduced [protected outcome] by [effect] under matched compute, while increasing compute alone [did/did not] recover the gain. The mechanism transferred to [domain] with [effect] and failed in [negative-control domain].

### Scientific validity layers test whether performance is real

Machine-native systems can exploit weaknesses in numerical methods, software oracles, semantic mappings, benchmarks or data pipelines. Every performance claim therefore binds, when material:

- scientific model adequacy;
- conditioning and numerical accuracy;
- implementation identity and precision;
- oracle/test sensitivity;
- uncertainty form;
- data and source provenance;
- semantic context;
- security/instruction–data boundary;
- governance and permitted use;
- benchmark/deployment relation.

**Primary result slot PF-R5.** Report how much apparent advantage survives validity hardening.

**Required sentence form:**

> After matching numerical accuracy, oracle adequacy, leakage controls and source permissions, the initial advantage changed from [a] to [b]. [Failure family] explained [portion] of the apparent gain.

### Machine-native search should improve scientific choice, not only final score

Protected scientific-transition outcomes include:

- correct unresolved state;
- discriminator selection;
- representation or problem-frame change;
- selective reopening;
- correct parent/source recovery;
- calibrated uncertainty;
- false completion;
- authority-respecting action;
- prospectively useful discovery.

**Primary result slot PF-R6.** Report transition-level quality rather than only benchmark score.

### Collaboration and local deference remain first-class

Hybrid arms allow humans to provide context, native semantics, physical observation, values or authority while machines provide search, memory, formal/numerical computation or branch management. The higher system should route to humans or parents when they are locally superior.

**Primary result slot PF-R7.** Report complementarity, handoff failures, expert burden and correct deference.

**Required sentence form:**

> The hybrid arm improved [outcome] through [division of labour], but suffered [handoff/tacit-state failure]. Correct routing to human or parent methods occurred in [x]% of local-deference cases.

### Robustness, adversaries and resource curves

Disturbances include shifted problem distributions, tool/model changes, prompt injection, poisoned retrieval, restricted data, reduced precision/compute and altered evaluator conditions. Results are presented as quality–cost–auditability surfaces, not one score.

**Primary result slot PF-R8.** Report resilience and failure frontier.

## Discussion

P-F proposes a move away from anthropomorphic evaluation without abandoning scientific accountability. A machine should not be rewarded because its internal process looks human, nor because it looks alien. It should be evaluated by the scientific transitions it enables, the evidence and validity that support them, the resources it uses and the failures it predicts.

The external-witness requirement is the key interface. Complete interpretability of internal computation may be impossible or unnecessary. Scientific adoption nevertheless needs an inspectable warrant proportionate to the claim. This allows machine-native intermediate representations while preserving a boundary at which independent science can accept, reject, reproduce or reopen the result.

The task strata are equally important. Human reasoning can be strongly advantageous when embodied skill, social meaning, ethics or institutional authority matters. Machine-native systems may be advantageous in combinatorial or high-dimensional spaces. Hybrid systems may dominate when these strengths are complementary. P-F should therefore produce a map of conditions rather than a winner.

The strongest threat is confounding. Machine-native arms often receive more compute, parallelism or engineering attention. They can exploit benchmark artifacts or hide weak numerical baselines. The paper’s central claim survives only after these advantages are measured and controlled. A pure engineering speedup may be valuable but is not by itself evidence for a new scientific-intelligence theory.

A second threat is witness burden. If producing reliable external evidence costs more than the machine-native gain, the architecture may not improve the scientific frontier. Conversely, some claims may require only compact certificates or source-bound evidence, making full human-like narration unnecessary. The experiment must measure this trade-off rather than assume it.

Negative outcomes remain informative. The best result may be a hybrid design, local human/parent superiority, or a conclusion that machine-native differences do not transfer. In those cases P-F should merge into P-C and the flagship as an implementation principle rather than persist as a standalone Article.

## Methods

### Architecture arms

1. SIMPLE/native domain algorithm;
2. HUMAN_EXPERT or expert team where feasible;
3. HUMAN_MIMETIC machine system using demonstrations/verbal reasoning structure;
4. MACHINE_NATIVE architecture;
5. HYBRID human–machine architecture;
6. expert-configured F0 parent federation;
7. F2 scientific-transition controller with machine-native implementation;
8. mechanism removals and parent replacements.

All arms share authorized sources, tools, evaluators and action permissions.

### Resource contracts

Bind:

- model/checkpoint and training/fine-tuning identity;
- inference compute and wall time;
- parallelism;
- memory and persistent artifacts;
- tool/software/instrument calls;
- numerical precision;
- human expert minutes and interventions;
- prompt/retrieval engineering labour;
- evaluation access;
- data/custody restrictions.

Use Pareto curves when exact matching is impossible.

### Task selection and leakage control

Task-design, architecture and evaluation teams are separated. Held-out domains and cases are selected after architectural hypotheses are frozen. Training contamination, public benchmark solutions, retrieval access and generated-case signatures are audited. Famous historical discoveries are secondary stress tests, not primary evidence.

### Witness evaluation

Independent domain adjudicators receive the witness and authorized artifacts without internal process traces unless the claim requires them. They assess correctness, scope, uncertainty, reproducibility and authority. Inter-rater dependence and source traditions are recorded.

### Outcomes

Primary:

- protected scientific-transition quality;
- critical false completion;
- resource-adjusted dominance by task stratum;
- independent witness sufficiency;
- causal mechanism effect;
- second-domain transfer.

Secondary:

- human/parent deference;
- collaboration burden;
- internal/external representation cost;
- robustness and security;
- calibration and uncertainty;
- failure prediction;
- implementation/failure surface.

### Analysis

Primary hypotheses and minimum effects are frozen by stratum. Stochastic systems receive repeated independent runs. Architecture comparisons model shared task and evaluator dependence. Hard integrity/authority failures are non-compensatory. No global intelligence rank is reported unless the evaluation universe and aggregation authority justify it.

## Limitations frozen before results

- “machine-native” is architecture- and hardware-relative;
- mechanism boundaries can be difficult to isolate;
- human-mimetic systems can also discover non-human strategies;
- task strata may reflect designer assumptions;
- witness adequacy can be domain- and institution-dependent;
- compute matching can be imperfect;
- cross-domain transfer may remain narrow;
- proprietary models and governed data can limit reproduction;
- no benchmark establishes general scientific intelligence.
