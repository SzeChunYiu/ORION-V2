## Human inquiry exceeds explicit reasoning

An executable science of inquiry cannot be built from explicit propositions and planned actions alone. Human problem solving is partly **reflexive**: people monitor their own uncertainty, confidence, errors and progress, then use those judgments to allocate effort, seek help or change strategy. Metacognitive confidence is itself an inference and can be miscalibrated, so reflection is not automatically a source of correctness. A machine-mediated research process therefore needs to distinguish the state of the scientific problem from its model of **how well its own inquiry is working**. Repeated self-critique generated by the same model should not be counted as independent validation; the relevant scientific question is whether self-monitoring is calibrated against outcomes and whether it selects useful changes in reasoning.

Human inquiry is also partly **tacit, embodied and distributed**. Ryle's distinction between knowing-how and knowing-that, Polanyi's account of tacit knowing, research on situated learning, and distributed-cognition studies all challenge the assumption that competent performance is fully specified by propositions held by one individual. A recipe may list ingredients and ordered operations yet omit the skilled perception needed to recognize texture, temperature, readiness or a recoverable deviation. A laboratory procedure can likewise be textually complete while competent execution still depends on instrument feel, local conventions or expert discrimination. In collaborative work, the relevant cognitive system can include people, notebooks, instruments, software, representations and social roles rather than one reasoning agent. For Machine Epistemics, this implies that procedural instructions, competence demonstrations and tacit dependencies are distinct scientific objects from declarative claims, and that reproducibility requires identifying when competent action depends on knowledge not captured by the written procedure.

Finally, human inquiry is partly **open ended**. Important questions can arise from an observation that was not sought for the current task. Peirce gave surprise a central role in abductive hypothesis generation; modern serendipity research distinguishes theory-led, observer-led, error-borne and network-emergent mechanisms; curiosity research distinguishes task-directed information sampling from broader information search. These traditions suggest a control problem that conventional objective-conditioned agents only partly capture. A research system should sometimes preserve unexpected observations that are distant from its current objective, ask whether they are relevant to another unresolved problem, and open a candidate inquiry when that relevance is defensible. Yet surprise is not evidence of importance and novelty is not authority. Stochastic noise, fashionable anomalies and accidental correlations provide essential negative controls.

These three properties—reflexivity, lived competence and open-ended encounter—extend the Machine-Epistemic Episode without changing its core question. They ask whether the system can represent not only what it currently believes and plans to do, but also **how reliable its own reasoning process is, which parts of competent action remain tacit or distributed, and whether an unexpected event indicates that the current problem representation or agenda is incomplete**. They also create new falsifiers. If ordinary metacognition, situated-learning models and curiosity/serendipity methods compose cleanly with the existing parent architecture, they should remain parent modules. A distinct Machine Epistemics contribution would require evidence that their interaction changes scientific-state decisions—when to seek an independent check, when a procedure is not reproducible despite textual completeness, when a failure should become a reusable lesson, or when an unexpected encounter justifies opening a new scientific question without prematurely treating it as discovery.
